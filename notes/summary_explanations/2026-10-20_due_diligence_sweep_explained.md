# The due-diligence sweep — what the new numbers mean (2026-10-20, second session)

Plain-language companion to `INT-41` (the control-book S-tier band), `INT-42` (the annual tier-E grid and the realised-curve reprice, with the reason a one-factor model cannot span the curve and what follows from it — `OQ-MKT-14`), `CCR-RISK-09` (the close-out collateral pairing) and `CCR-LIT-05` (the reference verification). Readings: [[2026-10-20_due_diligence_sweep]].

## The control-book S-tier band (`INT-41`)

**What was done.** The `INT-25` study — the ±1-tier jitter of the S matrix and the five structured variants (hidro_merged, fluvial_merged, flatten = $S^{1/2}$, sharpen = $S^2$, drop_alex) — ran on the control book with one rule added: every jittered or transformed table composes the *Mexican* denominator under the same transform, so the control γ stay on the Mexican absolute scale of each variant rather than being renormalised to 1 (`anchor.csv` logs $\sum w\gamma$ per variant: 0.27 hidro, 0.24 fluvial, 0.44 flatten, 0.08 sharpen, 0.24 drop_alex, 0.24 base).

**How to read the numbers.** The control book's headline EPE shift is $-3.50\,\%$ at base and spans $-5.91$ (flatten) to $-1.22\,\%$ (sharpen) across the variants; the Mexican book spans $-9.88$ to $-7.68\,\%$ on the same variants. The difference between the books — the heterogeneity statement of `INT-39` — is 5.50 pp at base and stays between 3.97 pp (flatten) and 6.46 pp (sharpen): the tier judgement moves both books in the same direction, and never closes the gap below 4 pp. Flatten and sharpen are the widest lever on the control book because its γ sit at the bottom of the ordinal ladder, where a square root or a square moves a tier the most in relative terms. The jitter ordering statistic is lower than the Mexican one (Kendall τ median 0.79, min 0.55, against 0.89 / 0.79) because the control γ profile is flatter and reorders more easily; it says the *ranking* of control names is soft, not that the book-level shift is.

**Why the rows keep their author tiers.** The two sector-exposure sources the canon named were verified and read: `[Bua2022]` ranks NACE Level-1 sections by their estimated beta to a text-based physical-risk index (Table 11) and `[ECB2021EconomyWide]` gives the share of high-physical-risk firms by NACE Level-1 sector (Chart 16) while concluding that physical risk is a property of location, not of sector. Neither is a sector exposure score vector, so the fourteen control rows remain author tiers `[eng]` with those two papers as the ordering references and the flatten / sharpen band as the honest width of that judgement — a limitations paragraph, not a data gap to fill.

## The annual tier-E grid and the realised-curve reprice (`INT-42`)

**The quantities.** *Initialisation date* — the past date at which the book is re-struck and the calibration re-fitted on pre-date data (BCBS 2010 ¶24 / ¶34); the grid is now every July from 2016 to 2025. *Non-overlapping sample* — for each horizon, the greedy subset of dates at least one horizon apart (¶13): all ten dates up to 1Y, every other year at 2Y, 2016 / 2019 / 2022 at 3Y (2025's 3Y window runs past the data). *Realised-curve reprice* — the same realised path (closes, FIX, F-TIIE) but with the rate legs discounting on the MXN zero curve stripped and Nelson–Siegel-fitted from the SIE quotes at each reporting date (313 strips, median lag 1 day, median NS rmse 11.5 bp) instead of on the model's own curve shape loaded on the realised short rate.

**How to read the model-mapped grid.** On the non-overlapping sample the baseline leg covers .52 / .79 / .94 of the realised netted MtMs at the nominal .50 / .80 / .95 — closer to nominal than the three-date record (.47 / .76 / .92) — with realised exposure at .97 of the forecast EE; the all-dates sample reads .47 / .75 / .89 because adjacent-year 2Y and 3Y windows over-weight the 2018–2021 rate cycle, which is exactly why ¶13 asks for the non-overlapping reading. The horizon profile is the record's: short horizons over-dispersed (1W .62 / .88 / .95), long horizons under-dispersed in the body (1Y .39 / .72 / .89, 2Y .37 / .59 / .92). The climate legs sit at PIT means .55 / .51 / .51 and A/E 1.02 / 1.00 / .99 for the headline / CT anchor / floor λ — the λ band brackets A/E = 1, as it did on three dates.

**How to read the realised-curve reprice.** At the book level the baseline reads .48 / .73 / .86 — lower than model-mapped at the 80 and 95 levels — and at 3Y it collapses to .11 / .30 / .59 against the model-mapped .33 / .67 / 1.00 (2Y .37 / .61 / .75 against .37 / .59 / .92). The mechanism: the model-mapped reprice values the realised path with the *same* curve mapping the forecast paths use (the HW1F $B(t,T)$ loading of the short rate), so it tests the short-rate forecast and nothing else; valued on the curve actually quoted, the IRS and bond legs feel the curve-*shape* moves of the period — the 2016–2019 flattening, the 2022–2024 inversion when Banxico sat at 11.25 % — which a one-factor model loading everything through the short rate cannot span, so the realised MtM lands outside the fan at the long horizons. It is the back-pricing twin of the tier-C finding that the one-factor rate cone collapses at 1Y (`INT-40`). The 0D pin is unchanged in order (median 12.6 MXN against 16.5; the dated calibration is its own strip), and the climate legs' A/E and band ordering are the same under either curve.

**What it means for the manuscript.** The `INT-40` "reasonable" verdict (¶38) stands as a statement about the exposure engine *given* the model's curve mapping; the realised-curve reading adds that the 2–3Y exposure of the rate legs is under-forecast on the observed curve — a limitation of the one-factor rate model, not of the climate channel, to be stated beside the tier-C cone. Two caveats remain documented: the option legs keep the flat fitted-σ surfaces (no implied-volatility history exists for Mexican single names), and the climate leg of every dated chain keeps the full-sample λ / γ / K windows (only `S_rate_eff` is re-inverted with the dated $a$), a ¶24 caveat on that leg.

### Why one factor cannot span the curve — the reason, and what follows from it

**The claim, unpacked.** In the Hull–White one-factor model the price at time $t$ of a zero-coupon bond maturing at $T$ is $P(t,T) = A(t,T)\,e^{-B(t,T)\,r_t}$ with $B(t,T) = (1 - e^{-a(T-t)})/a$ (`MKT-IR-01`, `[BrigoMercurio2006]` §3.3); $A$ is pinned by the initial curve through $\theta(t)$ (`MKT-IR-02`) and, after the initialisation date, the only thing that moves is the short rate $r_t$. Written as a zero rate, $z_t(\tau) = -\ln A(t,t+\tau)/\tau + \frac{B(\tau)}{\tau}\,r_t$: every tenor is the short rate times a fixed loading $B(\tau)/\tau$, equal to 1 at the short end and decaying with maturity. So the whole curve moves in **one fixed shape** — every tenor in the same direction as the short rate, by a fixed fraction of its move, with no change of curvature — on every path and at every date. A steepening, a flattening, an inversion or a hump is a move with a component orthogonal to that shape, and no value of $r_t$ produces it: it lies outside the model's state space. That is what "cannot span" means. It is a statement about the geometry of a one-factor model, not about how well $a$ and $\sigma$ were estimated — the empirical literature finds that bond-return variation needs three factors, level, slope and curvature (`[LittermanScheinkman1991]`), and a one-factor short-rate model carries only the first.

**How large the loading is here.** The dated chains calibrate on the TIIE-28 2001-start cell (`MKT-CALIB-06`), whose mean reversion runs from $a = 0.56$ (2016) to $0.26$ (2024), half-lives of 1.2 to 2.7 years; the frozen headline of the production run is $a = 0.0758$ (`MKT-CALIB-08`, half-life 9.1 years). At ten years the loading $B(10)/10$ is therefore 0.18–0.36 on the dated fans and 0.70 on the production fan. Two things follow for the back-pricing: the long end of a dated fan is nearly pinned (a 100 bp short-rate move carries 18–36 bp at ten years), and the width of the fan at tenor $\tau$ is the width of the short-rate fan scaled by $B(\tau)/\tau$ — the model's uncertainty about the ten-year rate three years out is a fifth to a third of its uncertainty about the short rate.

**What the two readings test.** Both use the same forecast fan (the HW1F paths from the pre-date calibration) and the same realised path (closes, FIX, F-TIIE). They differ only in how the *realised* MtM of the IRS, bond and FRN legs is computed at each reporting date. *Model-mapped:* the realised short rate is pushed through the same $A$, $B$ mapping the forecast uses, so the "realised" curve is the model's own curve at that short rate — the comparison asks whether $r$ landed inside the fan, and a curve-shape error cannot appear because both sides carry the model's shape. *Quoted curve:* the realised MtM is computed off the curve the market actually quoted, stripped and Nelson–Siegel-fitted from the SIE files at the reporting date (`yield_curve.curve_on`; 313 strips, median lag 1 day, median rmse 11.5 bp) — the comparison asks whether the *curve* landed inside the fan the model drew from $r$. The gap between the two readings is therefore exactly the slope and curvature content of the realised period, the part of the move the model has no coordinate for.

The two readings agree within a few points at one week and one month; from three months on the quoted-curve coverage of the 95 % band sits below the model-mapped one and the gap grows with the horizon (.97 → .90 at 3M, .89 → .82 at 1Y, .92 → .75 at 2Y, 1.00 → .59 at 3Y). At three years only 11 % of the realised MtMs fall inside the central half of the fan and 41 % fall outside a band built to miss 5 %. The A/E column is the other half of the finding — it is the same under both curves, .99 at three years: the *centre* of the fan is right on the observed curve (realised exposure matches the forecast EE), its *width* is not.

**Implication 1 — what tier E now validates.** The model-mapped grid (.52 / .79 / .94, A/E .97) validates the short-rate forecast and the exposure engine given the model's curve mapping, as `INT-40` stated; the quoted-curve grid shows that the term-structure forecast behind the rate legs is not validated beyond about one year. In the ¶38 sense the engine remains "reasonable" for horizons up to 1Y and for the mean exposure at every horizon; the honest sentence for the back-pricing section is that the 2–3Y rate-leg *dispersion* is under-forecast on the curve the market quoted, by the amounts in the table.

**Implication 2 — which exposure metrics are affected.** Because the centre is right and the width is wrong, the metrics that read the fan's centre survive and the metrics that read its tails do not. EE / EPE and anything built on them (CVA, the EPE deltas the thesis reports) are unaffected within the A/E .99–1.00 of the table; PFE-type quantities on the IRS and bond legs — potential future exposure at 95 / 99 %, limit utilisation, the tail of the netted MtM distribution — are too narrow beyond about one year, and a limit read off this fan's 95 % band at three years would be breached roughly eight times as often as designed (41 % against 5 %). The miss is not one-sided — an A/E of .99 with 41 % of the realisations outside the band means the realised curve sat on both sides of the fan at different dates — which is why it shows as a collapse of coverage rather than as a bias in the exposure.

**Implication 3 — the climate channel is untouched.** The thesis's headline number is a *difference* between a jump-on and a jump-off run that share the same rate model, the same paths and the same curve mapping; the curve-shape error is common to both runs and cancels in the difference. The evidence is in the table's twin for the climate legs: A/E per horizon differs by at most .01 between the two readings (headline leg 1.00 / 1.01 / 1.01 / 1.03 / 1.05 / 1.00 model-mapped against 1.00 / 1.01 / 1.01 / 1.04 / 1.04 / .99 on the quoted curve) and the λ-band ordering is the same, so the $-9.00 / -5.51 / -4.99\,\%$ EPE band does not depend on which curve the realised path is read on. One qualification belongs beside it: the rate jump reaches along the curve through the same loading $B(\tau)/\tau$ (the `INT-22` loading $L(a)$), so a climate shock that in reality moved the slope — a term-premium response, say — would be represented as a scaled parallel move; that is a known property of the rate leg, not a new finding.

**Implication 4 — the twin of the tier-C cone.** Tier C found the one-factor rate cone at .29 / .35 / .65 at one year (`INT-40`): the short rate's own distribution one year out is too narrow to follow a policy cycle, the rigidity *in time* of a single factor with fast reversion. The quoted-curve reading of tier E is the same rigidity *across maturity*: even where the short rate sits inside the fan, the curve does not. Both are properties of the model class, both are stated in the limitations beside each other, and neither is a property of the climate overlay.

**Implication 5 — the remedy and why it waits.** A second Gaussian factor (the two-factor Hull–White / G2++ of `[BrigoMercurio2006]` ch. 4) gives the curve a two-dimensional family of moves — two loadings with two reversion speeds — which spans slope changes; its calibration would use the curves themselves, and the 313 dated strips now written by the realised-curve reprice are exactly that data. It is post-thesis future work (`OQ-MKT-14`): the build is frozen (`CCR-SIM-03`), the pricers' curve mapping is the one-factor `SimulatedHW1FCurve` with the `discount_factors` hook as the only seam, and the two readings give the same climate A/E, so the change would re-read the baseline fan's width at the long tenors and at most re-read the climate band, not reorder it. The honest statement is the limitation, not the fix.

**The implied-volatility closure, and what it costs.** Part (b) of `OQ-INT-14` asked for the option legs to be repriced on market-implied volatilities. The reason it closed as infeasible is a data fact: no history of single-name implied volatilities exists for the Mexican names in the book — MexDer lists no usable single-stock surfaces, the project holds no vendor feed, and `data/market_mx/` carries FRED, SIE and Yahoo closes only. The consequence is that the equity-option legs' realised MtM is computed with the flat σ fitted from realised history at each date, the same σ the forecast uses, so the back-pricing of those legs tests the spot forecast and not the volatility (vega) channel; a change in implied volatility over the horizon would have moved the realised MtM and is invisible here, on both sides of the comparison. It is a limitation of the test, symmetric between forecast and realisation, confined to the option legs (one European option per surviving listed issuer on its own share, `configs/mexican_book.yaml`), and stated as such.

## The close-out collateral pairing (`CCR-RISK-09`)

The close-out date is the default date plus the margin period of risk; the exposure at close-out nets the collateral balance the counterparty held at the default date, because nothing is exchanged during the MPOR. The loop kept one "last balance seen" and, walking the simulation dates in order, reached the 2W default date (day 14) before the 0D / 1D / 1W close-outs (days 14 / 15 / 21), so those three netted the two-week balance. Each close-out now nets the balance frozen at its own default date. Only the collateralised 0D / 1D / 1W rows of the two VM counterparties of the fixture move (NAID 23 EE 46.07 → 45.99, PE99 51.96 → 51.12); no reported number moves because the results chain stores uncollateralised metrics, and the goldens' uncollateralised columns differ from the macOS-generated files only at the $10^{-13}$ cross-architecture level of `GEN-39`.

## The reference-verification record (`CCR-LIT-05`)

Thirty-eight of the forty keys in the to-confirm section were resolved on Crossref, the DOI handle API or the publisher's page and promoted into their sections, two of them into new sections for backtesting and for engineering / numerics; the twelve corrections are the kind a manuscript cannot carry (an author, a venue, pages, a year in a key). Two stay unverified for reasons outside the project — a mimeo with no serving copy, an insurer site that was down. `tools/check_refs.py` now resolves every DOI and URL of the bibliography into `literature/refs_check.csv`; its first run caught two wrong DOIs and one moved link in the legacy block, which is the argument for keeping the check in the appendix workflow.
